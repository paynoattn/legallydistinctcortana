The night train is back, and it is slower on purpose

Sleeper routes across Europe are full again. The people riding them are not in a hurry, and that is the point.

By Ines Kowalczyk. 11 November 2026. 9 minute read.

The 21:04 from Brussels to Prague leaves from platform 6, and by half past eight the platform is already full of people who have chosen to arrive tomorrow. There is a family with a cool box, a cellist with a berth booked for her instrument, and a man in a suit who explains, unprompted, that he has given up flying to meetings.

Ten years ago this train did not exist. Overnight services had been cut route by route across the continent, undercut by budget airlines and by the plain arithmetic of a sleeper car: it carries a third as many people as a day coach and spends its days parked in a siding.

What changed was not the trains but the passengers. A night train turns travel time into sleep time, and for a growing number of people that trade is worth more than speed. You board after dinner, wake up somewhere else, and step off in the middle of the city rather than at an airport an hour outside it. Operators say their busiest routes now sell out weeks ahead, even in November.

The trains themselves are a mixed bag. Some run new couchette cars with keycard locks and a USB-C socket in every berth; others still use carriages built in the 1980s, with windows that open and heating that does not always close. Regulars trade advice on which carriages to book and which to avoid, the way frequent fliers once traded seat maps.

Nobody claims the night train will replace the short-haul flight. The economics are still thin, track access at night competes with freight, and a single broken locomotive can strand four hundred people in a field outside Leipzig. But for a certain kind of trip, unhurried and city to city, it has become the obvious choice again.

At 21:04 exactly, the train pulls out. The cellist is asleep before Leuven.
